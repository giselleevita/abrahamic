**Status: drafts only. Nothing here is published or seeded.** The project rule is that

sit here for a qualified reviewer. Comparisons already have `sharedGround` and
`keyDifferences` fields that the page renders; approved text can be pasted in through

Each draft follows the site's method: say what each text says, name the source, and
do not say which is right.

## The Akedah: which son was offered? (`akedah-which-son`)

**Shared ground.** All three traditions remember a father asked to offer his son as a test
of faith, and a ram or sacrifice provided in the son's place.

**Why they differ.** Genesis 22 names Isaac. The Quran (37:99–113) tells the story
without naming the son. Early Islamic scholars held both views, and most later
Islamic tradition identifies the son as Ishmael. Christian tradition reads the story
as Isaac and often as a foreshadowing; Jewish tradition remembers it as the Akedah
(the binding of Isaac). The difference is mainly in which text is read and how it is
interpreted, not in the shape of the story.

## The death of Jesus (`death-of-jesus`, `was-jesus-crucified`)

**Shared ground.** All three traditions honour Jesus, and the Gospels and the Quran
both say that his enemies plotted against him.

**Why they differ.** The Gospels record that Jesus was crucified and, in Christian
belief, raised. The Quran (4:157–158) says it was not as people claimed, and
Muslim scholars have understood the verse in more than one way. Jewish tradition
does not regard Jesus as the Messiah. The traditions disagree about what happened,
and each reads its own text as authoritative.

## The Trinity (`the-trinity-debate`)

**Shared ground.** All three affirm that God is one and is the creator.

**Why they differ.** Christian doctrine, developed in the early councils, speaks of one
God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Judaism and Islam hold that God's
oneness is absolute and understand the doctrine as a departure from it (for Islam,
see Quran 4:171 and 5:73). Christians who hold the doctrine say it is a statement
of the same oneness. The word "Trinity" does not appear in the New Testament.

## The call to Abraham (`abrahams-call`)

**Shared ground.** Each tradition remembers Abraham as called by God to leave his
home and follow God, and as a model of faith.

**Why they differ.** Genesis 12 tells of a call to leave Haran for a land that
would be shown. The Quran emphasises Ibrahim's rejection of idols and his
submission to God (Quran 21:51–70; 3:67). Paul presents Abraham as a father of
those who have faith (Romans 4). Each tradition draws out what its own story needs.

## Circumcision (`circumcision-sign-covenant`)

**Shared ground.** Abraham is remembered as having been circumcised, and
circumcision has been practised among Jews and Muslims for many centuries.

**Why they differ.** Genesis 17 presents circumcision as the sign of the covenant
with Abraham's household, and Judaism keeps it as such. Christian teaching, in the
letters of Paul and the council described in Acts 15, does not require it of
non-Jewish believers. In Islam it is customary, with scholars differing on whether it
is required, and it is not set out in the Quran.

## The Messiah (`the-messiah-prophecy`)

**Shared ground.** The word means "anointed one", and all three traditions speak of
a coming deliverer or of a figure called the Messiah.

**Why they differ.** Jewish tradition looks for a future anointed king from David's line
who will restore Israel. Christians identify the Messiah (Christ) as Jesus. The
Quran calls Jesus "the Messiah" (al-Masih) while understanding him as a prophet and
messenger, and Islamic tradition also speaks of a future return of Jesus.
